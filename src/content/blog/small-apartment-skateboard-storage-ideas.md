Skateboards are the worst kind of clutter: too long for a shelf, too awkward for a closet, and too easy to trip over in the dark. One board leaning by the door turns into three, and before long every corner holds a deck and grip tape scratches the wall. These small apartment skateboard storage ideas fix that without turning your living room into a garage. Wall mounts, slim racks, and deck displays get boards off the floor and keep them visible, even in a studio. The 15 ideas below cover daily riders, collectors, and kids, including renter-safe options that leave no holes.

![Skateboards mounted on a pale wall in a small bright apartment living room](/images/small-apartment-skateboard-storage-ideas-hero.jpg)

## Count the decks: riders, collectors, and kids

Before you buy a mount or a rack, count what you actually own. A setup that holds two daily riders fails the moment a third deck, a cruiser, and a kid's board join the pile.

### 1. Count every deck, then sort by how often it gets ridden

Lay every board on the floor at once, including the one behind the door and the cruiser under the bed. Sort them into three groups: daily rider, weekly rider, and display or spare. A mount rated for two boards will not hold four.

### 2. Give each group its own zone

Daily riders live by the door, where you can grab one on the way out. Display decks go on the living room wall, where they earn their space. Kids' boards go low — about 24 inches off the floor — so a child can reach one without climbing.

<div class="tip"><strong>Measure first:</strong> a standard deck is about 31 to 32 inches long and 7.5 to 8.5 inches wide. Measure your longest board, then add 4 inches of clearance on each side for the mount hardware.</div>

## Wall mounts: the single best skateboard storage

Wall mounts are the single best skateboard storage for a small apartment because they use the one surface you are not already using: a deck on the wall takes up zero floor space.

### 3. Hang the daily rider on a two-hook wall mount

A two-hook mount — two padded hooks screwed into a stud about 18 inches apart — holds one complete board horizontally by its trucks. Mount it about 48 inches off the floor so the deck clears the skirting board and sits at grab height. Use padded hooks: bare metal scratches trucks and chips deck edges.

### 4. Store several decks vertically with a wall-mounted deck rack

A vertical wall rack holds three to five decks upright, side by side, in a footprint about 12 inches wide. Each deck slides into its own slot, nose down, so you can pull one out without disturbing the rest. Mount it so the tails clear the floor by at least 2 inches.

## Deck displays that double as wall art

A good deck graphic deserves to be seen. Deck displays turn storage into decoration, which is exactly what a small apartment needs.

### 5. Float one deck on a hidden deck display mount

A hidden mount grips the deck from behind, so the board looks like it floats a couple of inches off the wall with no visible hardware. Mount it at eye level — about 60 inches from the floor to the center of the deck — above a console table, a sofa, or a desk.

### 6. Build a rotating display wall for the collection

Collectors rarely ride every deck they own, so pick three or four favorites for the wall and store the rest. A row of simple floating mounts lets you swap decks in and out in under a minute, so the display changes with the season or whenever a new deck arrives. The same rotation habit works for any collection — see our [16 Small Apartment Storage Ideas for Collectors](/blog/small-apartment-storage-ideas-collectors/) for how to display a few favorites and protect the rest.

## Slim floor racks for the daily rider

Not every board belongs on the wall. The board you ride every day should be the easiest to grab, and a slim floor rack does that without drilling.

### 7. Use a slim freestanding rack by the door

A freestanding rack holds two to four boards upright in a footprint of about 14 by 10 inches — roughly the size of a doormat. Place it just inside the front door, on the side your hand naturally reaches. Choose one with rubber feet and padded slots so it does not slide or chip deck edges. The same upright, grab-and-go logic works for other wheeled gear — our [15 Bike Storage Ideas for Small Apartments](/blog/bike-storage-ideas-small-apartments/) shows how vertical stands keep a bike ready by the door in the same footprint.

### 8. Slide a low rack under a console table or bench

A low horizontal rack under a console table, bench, or entryway shelf holds one or two decks flat and out of sight. It needs about 4 inches of clearance height for a complete board with wheels, so measure the gap before you buy. Slide the board in nose-first so the tail — the part you grab — sticks out just enough to pull.

## Wheels, trucks, and tools: the small-parts zone

Decks get all the attention, but the small parts cause most of the mess. A single loose wheel can roll under a radiator and vanish for a month. Give wheels, trucks, bearings, and tools one small zone and the clutter disappears.

### 9. Corral wheels, bearings, and hardware in one labeled parts bin

Use a small stackable parts bin — about 6 by 4 by 3 inches per compartment — with a lid, and label each compartment: wheels, bearings, bolts, bushings. Keep the bin on one shelf, always in the same place, so you never hunt for a bearing again.

### 10. Mount a small pegboard tool station above the parts bin

A pegboard about 18 by 24 inches above your parts bin holds the skate tool, Allen keys, spare bearings, and a rag on hooks, all visible at a glance. Hang the tools you reach for weekly at the front and the rarely used spares at the back.

## Renter-safe mounts that hold without drilling

A lease that bans drilling does not ban skateboard storage. Renter-safe mounts hold a deck securely and come down cleanly at move-out — if you respect their weight limits and prep the surface.

### 11. Use heavy-duty adhesive hooks rated above the deck's weight

A complete board weighs about 5 to 7 pounds, so choose adhesive hooks rated for at least 10 pounds per hook and use two hooks per board. Clean the wall with rubbing alcohol, let it dry, press the hook firmly for 30 seconds, and wait the full curing time on the packet — usually 24 hours — before hanging anything.

### 12. Lean a no-drill floor rack against the wall

A leaning rack — a freestanding frame that rests against the wall and holds boards in padded slots — needs no screws, no adhesive, and no permission. Put a small rubber mat under its feet so it cannot slide on hard floors, and check that the top leans firmly against the wall with no gap. The whole setup moves with you in one piece.

<div class="tip"><strong>Renter rule:</strong> before any mount goes up, read your lease for the exact wording on holes and adhesive. Photograph the wall before and after, so you have proof at move-out.</div>

## Keeping boards dry and ding-free in small spaces

Small apartments punish boards in small ways: damp corners, knocks from chairs, and grip tape grinding against other decks.

### 13. Dry the deck and trucks after every wet ride

Wipe the deck, trucks, and bearings dry as soon as you come in, and leave the board upright for an hour so water drains off the bearings. Never store a wet board flat in a closet — trapped moisture rusts bearings and softens the deck. The same rule applies to other board gear: the drying routine in our [16 Small Apartment Ski and Snowboard Storage Ideas](/blog/small-apartment-ski-snowboard-storage-ideas/) is dry first, store second.

### 14. Slip a cover over the nose and tail between rides

A simple deck cover, or even an old sock slipped over the nose and tail, stops the two most common dings: chips from bumping the wall and scratches from sliding against another deck. Covers also keep grip tape from snagging clothes and scratching furniture when a board leans against a chair.

### 15. Rotate the display and check the hardware once a month

Once a month, take each stored board down, check that the truck bolts are tight, spin each wheel to catch a seized bearing, and wipe the deck clean. Rotate the display decks at the same time so no single board sits in a sunny spot for months.

## Mistakes to avoid

Do not buy a mount before measuring your longest deck — a rack that is 2 inches too short is useless. Do not stack decks grip-side to grip-side: grip tape grinds the graphic off the deck below and traps grit between boards. Do not store boards in the bathroom or against a damp outside wall; moisture is the quiet killer of decks and bearings. Do not hang a board above a radiator or in a sunny window, where heat and light dry out the wood and fade the graphic. And do not hang a whole board on one weak adhesive hook — a falling board can chip a deck and dent a floor.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I store a skateboard in a small apartment without drilling holes?

Use a freestanding floor rack by the door, a leaning rack that rests against the wall, or heavy-duty adhesive hooks rated for at least 10 pounds per hook. All three leave no marks, and the freestanding options move with you.

### Can I hang a skateboard on the wall?

Yes. A two-hook wall mount screwed into a stud holds a complete board safely, and a vertical wall rack holds three to five decks in about 12 inches of wall width. If you cannot drill, use adhesive hooks rated above the board's weight or a leaning rack instead.

### How do I store multiple skateboards in a tiny room?

Store the daily rider on a wall mount or floor rack by the door, display three or four favorites on the wall, and keep the rest in a vertical rack or under a bench. Rotate the display decks monthly so every board gets seen.

### Should I store my skateboard vertically or horizontally?

Both work. Vertical storage saves the most floor and wall space, which is why it suits tiny rooms. Horizontal storage shows off the deck graphic better and is easier to grab. Choose vertical for the boards you ride and horizontal for the ones you display — either way, keep the board dry.

## Final thoughts

Fifteen ideas, one rule: every board gets a spot off the floor, and the spot you use most is the one closest to the door. Start with the count, mount the daily rider, and add the display and the parts zone as you go.
