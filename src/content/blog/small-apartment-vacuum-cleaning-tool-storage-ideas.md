A vacuum cleaner is the hardest thing to store in a small apartment: tall, top-heavy, and awkwardly shaped, with a hose, a cord, and attachments that never stay put. Brooms and mops are no better — they slide over and end up leaning by the front door. These small apartment vacuum storage ideas give every tool a real home, even with almost no spare space. From wall hooks and behind-door racks to slim cabinets and renter-safe mounts, every idea uses space you already have. The 17 ideas below work in rentals, need little or no drilling, and most cost less than a takeout dinner.

## Measure the tools first: the vacuum's real footprint (and why closets fail)

A standard upright is roughly 42 to 45 inches tall, 12 to 14 inches wide, and 11 to 13 inches deep, and the hose sticks out past those numbers. A stick vacuum is slimmer but often taller, around 44 to 48 inches. Measure your vacuum, then the spot, leaving 2 inches of clearance on each side.

**1. Measure height, width, and depth — including the hose.** Keep the three numbers in your phone. Every hook, cabinet, and rack you consider has to beat all three, not just one.

**2. Find the real reason your closet fails.** In most small apartments, the closet is not too small — it is too full. Before you buy anything, pull everything out and check the floor space left. The vacuum may already fit once the clutter is gone.

## Closet door and wall hooks: hanging the vacuum upright

Hanging the vacuum turns dead wall space into storage. An upright hangs from a hook about 36 to 40 inches high, handle in the hook and base on the floor. A stick vacuum needs a wall mount or a hook near the top.

**3. Hang the upright vacuum from a heavy-duty wall hook.** A single J-hook rated for at least 15 pounds holds most upright vacuums. Mount it into a stud, or use a toggle bolt.

**4. Use a stick vacuum wall mount with a charging dock.** Most stick vacuums come with a wall mount, many with a charging dock. Mount it inside the closet or on a hallway wall, base 4 to 6 inches off the floor.

**5. Add a small hook for the hose and attachments.** The hose is what makes a stored vacuum look messy. A small hook beside the vacuum holds the hose in a loop, and a mesh bag on the same hook keeps the crevice tool and brush head together. ## Behind-door racks for brooms, mops, and dustpans

The back of a door is wasted space in almost every apartment, and it is exactly the right shape for long-handled tools. A behind-door rack keeps brooms and mops vertical and reachable without taking floor or shelf space. Measure the door thickness first.

**6. Mount a grip-style broom holder on the back of a closet door.** Spring-loaded rubber grips hold handles of different thicknesses and release them with a quick pull. A three-grip holder, about 12 to 16 inches wide, fits a broom, a mop, and a duster side by side. Mount it about 48 inches high so the heads clear the floor.

**7. Hang a slim over-the-door rack for the dustpan and hand tools.** A narrow wire rack that hooks over the door holds the dustpan, a hand brush, spray bottles, and cloths in one column. Keep the depth under 4 inches so the door still closes fully. For more door-storage ideas, see [Over-the-Door Organization Ideas for Every Room](/blog/over-the-door-organization-ideas/).

## Slim cabinets and tall narrow storage that looks like furniture

When you have no closet to spare, a slim cabinet does the job and looks like furniture. The best options are 10 to 14 inches deep — deep enough for a vacuum, shallow enough not to block a walkway. Choose one with a full-height door and no fixed shelves.

**8. Use a tall narrow cabinet as a vacuum garage.** A pantry-style cabinet about 15 inches wide and 70 inches tall swallows an upright vacuum, a broom, and a mop. Remove the middle shelf if there is one, and add a small hook inside the door for the dustpan.

**9. Hide the vacuum behind a freestanding divider or curtain.** If a cabinet is not in the budget, a slim tension rod with a floor-length curtain across an awkward alcove or the gap beside a wardrobe creates a hidden vacuum nook. Pick a curtain close to your wall color.

## The cleaning closet: turning a sliver of space into a station

You do not need a full closet to have a cleaning station. A sliver of space — the gap beside the washer, the end of a hallway, a 12-inch strip by the front door — can hold everything if you build upward. The goal: grab the vacuum, broom, and spray bottle in one motion.

**10. Build a vertical cleaning station in a 12-inch gap.** Mount two rows of hooks and one grip holder: the vacuum hangs below, brooms and mops grip above, and a small shelf at eye level holds sprays and cloths.

**11. Keep a small shelf at eye level for daily supplies.** One 10-inch-deep shelf above the vacuum holds all-purpose spray, glass cleaner, and microfiber cloths. When everyday supplies live at eye level and bulky tools live below, one quick cleanup needs just one stop. ## Ironing board and step stool: the flat-and-tall problem, solved

The ironing board is the forgotten member of the cleaning-tool family. It is flat, wide, and almost 5 feet long, so it does not fit where the vacuum fits. The step stool is small but oddly shaped, with no home of its own.

**12. Hang the ironing board flat against the inside of a closet door.** An over-the-door holder, or two hooks spaced about 24 inches apart, holds a standard folding board vertically against the door. The board's own hook catches on the holder, and it sits less than 3 inches off the door.

**13. Store the step stool on a hook or on top of the vacuum cabinet.** A folding step stool hangs from a single large hook inside the closet, or lives flat on top of a tall cabinet.

## The grab-and-go caddy: daily tools stay out, bulky tools stay hidden

Not every cleaning tool needs to be hidden. The tools you use every day — a spray bottle, cloths, a dustpan — should be easy to grab, while the bulky vacuum and mop stay tucked away. A grab-and-go caddy is the bridge: one portable carrier for the daily tools.

**14. Keep one caddy for daily cleaning and nothing else.** A small handled caddy, roughly 12 by 8 inches, holds one all-purpose spray, one glass cleaner, two microfiber cloths, and a scrub brush. Park it under the kitchen sink or by the door — when it is time to clean, you carry one thing, not five.

**15. Give the caddy a fixed parking spot with a label.** A caddy without a home ends up on the counter. Mark its spot with a small label or a tray it always sits on. If more than one person cleans, the label keeps the system alive.

<div class="tip"><strong>The two-minute rule:</strong> If putting the vacuum away takes more than two minutes, you will stop doing it. Pick the spot closest to where you actually clean.</div>

## Renter-safe mounting that holds real weight

Renters often skip wall mounting because they fear losing the deposit, but several renter-safe options hold real weight. Match the fastener to the load and wall type: adhesive strips work on smooth painted walls and tile, tension rods work between two solid surfaces, and over-the-door hardware needs no holes.

**16. Use heavy-duty adhesive hooks rated above your vacuum's weight.** Large adhesive strips and hooks rated for 7 to 15 pounds hold a stick vacuum, a broom holder, or a dustpan. Clean the wall with rubbing alcohol, press firmly for 30 seconds, and wait a full hour before hanging anything. On textured walls or brick, adhesive fails — use a tension-mounted or over-the-door option instead.

**17. Use a tension rod or a freestanding rack when walls are off-limits.** A tension rod wedged across a closet or an alcove gives you a hanging rail for sprays and hooks for tools, with zero holes. A freestanding narrow rack, about 12 inches wide, does the same job and moves with you when your lease ends.

## Mistakes to avoid

**Buying storage before measuring.** The most common mistake is ordering a cabinet, rack, or hook set and discovering the vacuum is two inches too tall. Measure first, buy second.

**Storing the vacuum with a full dustbin or a wet mop head.** A full bin smells within days, and a damp mop head grows mildew. Empty the vacuum and let mop heads dry before they go back.

**Hanging heavy tools from weak adhesive.** A broom weighs little, but a full-size upright vacuum does not belong on a small adhesive hook. Match the rating to the tool, and use a stud or toggle bolt over 10 pounds.

**Hiding the tools so well you never use them.** Storage that takes five minutes to undo is storage you will abandon. If the vacuum lives behind three other things, move it to the front.

**Forgetting the cord and attachments.** A vacuum with its cord trailing across the floor is not stored — it is dropped. Wrap the cord on its hooks and keep the attachments in one bag or pouch.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How can I store a vacuum in a small apartment with no closet?
Use a slim tall cabinet, a behind-door rack, or a wall hook. A stick vacuum on a wall mount takes almost no floor space, and a curtain across a small alcove hides an upright vacuum with no building work.

### Can I hang a vacuum on the wall in a rental?
Yes. Use heavy-duty adhesive hooks rated above the vacuum's weight, a stick vacuum wall mount, or an over-the-door rack that needs no holes. Check your lease for mounting rules and use removable fasteners where required.

### How do I store brooms and mops so they do not fall over?
Store them vertically in a grip-style holder, which clamps the handle and releases it with a pull. Leaning brooms against a wall always ends with them on the floor.

### Can a stick vacuum replace an upright in a small apartment?
For most small apartments, yes. A stick vacuum is lighter, slimmer, and easier to store on a wall mount, and it handles hard floors and low-pile rugs well. Keep an upright only for thick carpet throughout.
