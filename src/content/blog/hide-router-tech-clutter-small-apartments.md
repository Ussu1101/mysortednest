The blinking black box on your bookshelf is doing more damage to your living room than the pile of laundry ever will. Routers, modems, charging cables, and streaming boxes are the clutter a small apartment can't seem to shake — and unlike a messy drawer, you can't just shut a door on them. If you want to hide router small apartment clutter without killing your Wi-Fi, the trick is not to bury the router. It is to give it a home that hides it from your eyes while keeping it open to air and signal. These 15 ideas do exactly that: ventilated boxes, bookshelf camouflage, wall mounts, and cable routes that make the tech disappear and keep your connection fast.

## The router rules: airflow and signal beat hiding every time

Before you hide anything, learn the two rules every other idea here depends on. First, a router makes heat and cools itself through vents on the top and sides. Block those vents and it overheats, slows down, and restarts at random. Second, Wi-Fi passes easily through wood, fabric, and drywall, but struggles through metal, mirrors, tile, and water — including a fish tank. So the goal is a spot that looks closed and stays open.

### 1. Run a 60-second speed test before you hide anything

Open a free speed-test app on your phone, stand where you usually sit, and write down the download number. Hide the router, wait five minutes, and test again from the same spot. If the number drops by more than about 15 percent, the spot is too closed — add airflow or use a thinner cover.

<div class="tip"><strong>Metal is the enemy:</strong> never hide a router in a metal box, a metal filing cabinet, or behind a mirrored door. Metal blocks Wi-Fi almost completely, and a router in a metal box is a router that barely works.</div>

## Ventilated router boxes that actually work

### 2. Use a slatted wooden router box

Look for a bamboo or acacia box with slats, a lattice front, or a perforated lid, at least 12 inches wide, 9 inches deep, and 5 inches tall — most routers are about 8 by 6 inches, so that leaves roughly 2 inches of air on every side. Set the router upright with its vents facing up, run the cables out through the open back, and put the box on a shelf at waist height or above. It costs roughly $20 to $35 and barely slows the signal, because wood and open slats hardly block Wi-Fi.

### 3. Hide it in a woven basket with a false back

A lidded seagrass or rattan basket with a loose weave works the same way and costs less. Choose one about 14 inches long, cut a small cable opening in the back, and set the router on a folded hand towel inside so it sits level and the bottom vents stay clear. Skip baskets lined with plastic or foil inside — the lining traps heat and blocks signal.

## Bookshelf camouflage: hiding the router in plain sight

### 4. Camouflage the router between a row of books

Stand the router upright at the end of a row of books, front edge flush with the spines, with about a 1-inch gap between the router and the nearest book. Choose books close to the router's height — around 8 to 10 inches tall — so its top edge lines up with the spines instead of poking above them. Never sandwich it between two tight rows of books, or the heat has nowhere to go.

### 5. Put the router in a decorative storage box on the shelf

A linen or canvas storage box, roughly 12 by 10 by 8 inches, works the same way. Cut a 2-inch cable slot in the back with a craft knife, set the router inside on a paperback book as a riser, and leave the lid off or ajar by about an inch so warm air escapes. ## Wall-mounting the router above eye level

### 6. Mount the router on a small floating shelf

Install a floating shelf about 5 to 6 feet high, near an outlet and roughly in the center of the apartment — the wall between the living room and bedroom is often ideal. A shelf 10 inches wide and 6 inches deep holds most routers. In a rental, use two small screws; the holes are about the size of a picture hook and easy to fill when you move out.

### 7. Hang the router on a wall bracket or keyhole mount

Many routers have keyhole slots on the back, made for exactly this. Drive one or two small screws into the wall, leaving the heads out by about a quarter inch, and slide the router onto them so it hangs flat with its vents facing out. If yours has no keyholes, a universal wall bracket — a flat plate with a lip, about $8 to $12 — does the same job.

## The charging drawer: phones, cables, and clutter in one place

### 8. Convert a drawer into a charging drawer

Pick a drawer in a console table, nightstand, or desk, ideally within 3 feet of an outlet. Drill a 1.5-inch hole in the back panel and press in a plastic grommet so cable edges stay smooth. Put a 6-outlet power strip inside, run its cord out through the grommet, and add a few adhesive clips to hold each cable in its own lane. For a full walkthrough, see our guide to [15 Family Charging Station Ideas for Small Homes (End the Cable Chaos)](/blog/family-charging-station-ideas-small-homes/).

### 9. Set up a nightstand charging drawer with labeled cables

A bedroom version works the same way on a smaller scale: one drawer, one 3-outlet strip, one cable per device. Label each cable at the plug end with masking tape — "phone," "watch," "earbuds" — so nobody unplugs the wrong one in the dark. Leave the drawer open about an inch overnight so warm air escapes.

<div class="tip"><strong>One strip, one drawer:</strong> never daisy-chain two power strips together inside a drawer. One strip, plugged directly into the wall, is the safe limit — and it is all a charging drawer needs.</div>

## Cable channels and cord covers that disappear

### 10. Run cables in paintable raceway channels

A raceway is a slim plastic channel, about 1 inch wide, that sticks to the wall with adhesive backing and snaps shut over the cables inside. Run it vertically from the router or TV to the baseboard, then horizontally to the outlet. Paint it the same color as the wall and it disappears from more than a few feet away. A 4-foot length costs roughly $8 to $12.

### 11. Bundle loose cables in a sleeve behind furniture

Where cables run behind a sofa, desk, or console, use a fabric cable sleeve — a zip-up tube about 1 inch wide that gathers three or four cables into one neat cord. Secure it every 18 inches with an adhesive clip and let the furniture hide the rest. ## Hiding the TV box, console, and streaming gear

### 12. Put the gear in a ventilated media cabinet with mesh doors

Choose a low media cabinet with mesh, cane, or slatted doors instead of solid wood or glass. Infrared remotes work through mesh and cane, so the doors can stay closed while you watch, and the open weave lets heat out. Inside, give each device at least 2 inches of space above it and cut a cable slot in the back panel for every shelf.

### 13. Mount the streaming stick and console behind the TV

A streaming stick can plug straight into the TV's HDMI port and hide behind the screen — add a short USB power cable and a small adhesive clip to hold it flat. For a game console, use a VESA-compatible wall mount or an under-shelf bracket so it hangs vertically behind or beside the TV, out of sight from the sofa. ## The 10-minute tech audit: what can leave the apartment

### 14. Apply one-in, one-out to chargers and cables

Gather every charger and cable in the apartment into one box and test each one against the device it belongs to. Anything that fits no device you still own goes into a recycling bag — most electronics stores take old cables for free. The rest goes into a labeled "spares" pouch, one per cable type, kept in the charging drawer.

### 15. Retire the range extender you no longer need

Many apartments have a Wi-Fi extender left over from a previous router or a dead spot that no longer exists. Unplug it for one week and watch whether any room loses signal. If nothing changes, it was only adding a blinking box, a cable, and an outlet hog. If a dead spot appears, move the router 3 to 5 feet toward that room first — a better position usually beats an extra device.

## Mistakes to avoid

- **Sealing the router in a closed cabinet.** A solid-door cabinet with no ventilation overheats the router and can cut your speed in half.
- **Hiding the router behind the TV.** The TV's metal back and electronics interfere with the signal.
- **Burying the router under books or clothes.** It looks tidy for a day, then the router overheats.
- **Coiling spare cable into tight loops.** Tight coils trap heat and stress the cable — use loose figure-eight loops with a Velcro tie instead.
- **Putting the router where you can't reach the reset button.** Every hiding spot should let you reach the router in under ten seconds.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I put my router in a closed cabinet?

Only if the cabinet has real airflow — a mesh door, ventilation holes, or an open back. A solid closed cabinet traps heat and weakens the signal. A ventilated box or open-backed shelf is safer.

### Will hiding my router slow down my Wi-Fi?

It can, but it doesn't have to. Wood, fabric, wicker, and thin plastic barely affect the signal; metal, mirrors, tile, and thick stacks of books do. In a ventilated wooden box or behind books with gaps for air, the difference is usually too small to notice.

### How can I hide router cables in a rental without drilling?

Use adhesive-backed raceway channels along the baseboard, fabric cable sleeves behind furniture, and adhesive clips every 18 inches. All three peel off cleanly, especially if you warm the adhesive with a hair dryer first.

### Where is the best place to put a router in a small apartment?

High and central — about 5 feet off the floor, near the middle of the apartment, out in the open or inside a ventilated cover. Keep it away from the TV, microwave, mirrors, and thick walls.

## Conclusion

Hiding your router and tech clutter is not about burying the gear — it is about giving every device a ventilated, reachable home and every cable a covered route. Start with one idea, test your speed before and after, and add the next one only when the first is working.
