Every January, the same problem shows up in a small apartment: the tree comes down, the ornaments come off, and suddenly a six-foot tree needs a home for eleven months where there is barely room for a vacuum. The best small apartment Christmas tree storage ideas solve two problems at once — keeping the branches from getting crushed and bent, and finding a spot that does not eat the space you live in every day. This guide does both: measure first, pick the right bag or box, and use the hidden spots your apartment already has — under the bed, on the closet shelf, behind the sofa — so next December starts with decorating, not untangling.

![A Christmas tree storage bag beside a sofa in a bright apartment living room](/images/small-apartment-christmas-tree-storage-ideas-hero.jpg)

## Measure the tree first: height, width, and branch type decide the container

Measure before you buy: a bag two inches too short bends the top branches, and one a foot too wide wastes space.

### 1. Measure height, width, and branch length before you shop

Measure the tree from floor to tip, the widest branch spread, and the longest single branch. Most 6 to 7.5-foot trees need a bag or box rated for at least 7.5 feet and 30 to 36 inches wide with the branches folded in.

### 2. Sort the tree by branch type: hinged, hook-on, or pencil

Hinged-branch trees fold flat against the trunk and compress into a slim bag, while hook-on branches come off one by one and need layering in old sheets so the hooks do not snag. Pencil trees are narrow enough for a standard upright bag or a tall wardrobe box.

<div class="tip"><strong>Measure twice, buy once:</strong> add two inches to your longest measurement. A bag with room to spare is easy to zip; an exact fit will fight you every January.</div>

## Tree storage bags: the flat, slide-under-the-bed winner

For most apartments, a flat storage bag is the best answer: cheap, light, and made to slide under the bed.

### 3. Use a flat, zippered tree bag with reinforced handles

Look for heavy-duty polyethylene or woven polypropylene, with a full-length zipper and handles on both ends. A good flat bag for a 7.5-foot tree runs roughly 48 to 60 inches long and 15 to 20 inches wide.

### 4. Compress branches with straps or old belts before zipping

Fold the branches up toward the tip, then wrap two or three straps — or old belts — around the bundle. This keeps the tree slim enough to slide under a bed and stops branches from springing open and snagging the zipper.

## Upright tree bags for closets and corners

If your tree is four feet or under, an upright bag keeps it standing and ready to decorate next year.

### 5. Stand a small tree upright in a vertical bag and wheel it into the closet

An upright tree bag is a tall, zippered cover that slips over the tree and cinches at the base, often with wheels built in. It works best for trees up to 5 feet tall — anything taller will not clear a standard 80-inch closet door — so roll it into the back of a coat closet and hang coats in front of it.

## Hard boxes vs soft bags: when the extra protection pays

Soft bags are light and cheap; hard boxes protect better — the choice depends on what will sit on top of the tree.

### 6. Choose a hard-sided box when the tree shares space with heavy items

If your storage spot is anywhere boxes get stacked, a hard box earns its price. A rigid plastic box with latches, sized to your longest tree section, stops a stack of winter boots or a suitcase from flattening the top branches — though it costs roughly three to five times a soft bag and cannot be folded away when empty.

### 7. Use a soft bag when the tree gets its own protected spot

If the tree will slide under the bed or stand alone in a closet corner where nothing stacks on top of it, a soft bag is all you need. Spend the savings on a thicker weave and a real zipper instead of a drawstring, and wrap the tree in an old flat sheet first — it stops branches poking through and keeps dust off the needles. ## Under-bed storage: the hidden square footage

A standard queen bed hides roughly 30 cubic feet underneath — the biggest storage zone you already own.

### 8. Slide the bagged tree under the bed with a dust cover on top

Most platform beds offer 6 to 8 inches of clearance, beds on risers 10 to 12, and a flat-packed 7.5-foot tree is usually 6 to 10 inches tall — so measure before you buy the bag. Slide the bag in handle-first so you can pull it out by the handle next December.

### 9. Raise the bed with risers to create clearance for a full-size tree

If your bed sits too low, a set of bed risers adds 3 to 6 inches of clearance for about the price of a pizza. Choose sturdy plastic or wood risers rated for your bed's weight, and lift all four legs evenly — never raise only the foot of the bed. ## Closet top shelf: the out-of-sight, out-of-mind zone

The top shelf of a closet is the easiest place to forget a tree for eleven months — which is exactly the point.

### 10. Store the bagged tree flat on the closet top shelf, label facing out

Lay the bagged tree flat on the top shelf of your least-used closet. Put the label facing out, and keep the tree away from the shelf edge, where it could slide off.

## Ornament and light storage that rides along with the tree

The tree is only half the job — ornaments and lights packed elsewhere are why decorating takes three hours instead of one.

### 11. Pack ornaments in divided bins that stack on the tree bag

Use a divided ornament box or a bin with cardboard dividers, one ornament per cell, wrapped in tissue if it is glass. A 12 x 12-inch divided box holds roughly 25 to 30 small ornaments and stacks neatly on top of a flat tree bag under the bed or on a closet shelf.

### 12. Wind each light strand on a spool and store it in the same zone

Wind each light strand around a spool, a piece of cardboard, or an empty paper-towel tube, and secure the ends with a twist tie. Test every strand before you pack it, replace any dead bulbs now, then store the spools in a small bin beside the tree bag.

## Labeling and the 10-minute pack-up routine

Storage systems fail in year two, not year one — labels and a short routine keep the system working.

### 13. Label every container with contents, tree size, and the year

Label the tree bag, the ornament box, and the light bin with three things: what is inside, the tree size, and the year. Use a luggage tag or masking tape with a marker — both survive eleven months in a closet — and if the tree fills more than one container, number them "Tree 1 of 2" and "Tree 2 of 2".

### 14. Follow a 10-minute pack-up routine the day the tree comes down

Do not leave the tree standing for a week after the holidays — it only collects dust and gets in the way. Set a timer for ten minutes: strip the ornaments into their divided box, wind the lights onto spools, fold the branches up, strap the tree, and zip the bag.

<div class="tip"><strong>Pack it the same day:</strong> the tree comes down, the ornaments come off, and the bag gets zipped in one session. A tree that sits undecorated in a corner for a week never gets packed properly.</div>

## What NOT to store with the tree (moisture, heat, pests)

Where you store the tree matters as much as how you pack it: moisture, heat, and pests are all avoidable.

### 15. Keep the tree out of damp, hot, and pest-prone spots

Never store the tree in a damp basement corner, on a balcony that gets rain, or against an exterior wall that sweats in summer — moisture grows mold and rusts the hinges. Never store it by a radiator or water heater, or in an attic that bakes above 100°F in summer, and never store it loose where mice nest — a sealed bag is the simplest pest barrier. If you do have attic space, read [17 Small Attic Storage Organization Ideas](/blog/small-attic-storage-organization-ideas/) first, because attics need ventilation and temperature checks before anything delicate goes up there.

## Mistakes to avoid

The fastest way to ruin a tree is to pack it wet, crush it under heavy boxes, or forget which bag holds it. Never pack a tree that is still damp — let it dry overnight first, or mold will grow inside the bag. Never stack anything heavy on a soft tree bag — switch to a hard box or move the tree. Never toss loose ornaments into the same bag as the tree, and never store the tree without a label — by next December, every black bag in your closet looks identical.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I store a Christmas tree in a small apartment without a garage?

Yes. A flat tree bag slides under the bed, an upright bag stands in a closet corner, and a bagged tree fits flat on a closet top shelf — most apartments have at least one of these spots.

### How do I store a Christmas tree in a small space without crushing the branches?

Fold the branches up toward the tip, strap the bundle tight, and pack it in a bag or box that fits with two inches to spare. Store it flat, with nothing stacked on top — and if heavy items share the shelf, use a hard-sided box instead.

### Can I leave my Christmas tree assembled all year?

You can with a tall closet and an upright tree bag with wheels — trees up to 5 feet tall work best. Taller trees usually will not clear a standard closet door, so they need to come apart and go into a flat bag.

### What is the best container for Christmas tree storage in an apartment?

A flat, zippered storage bag is best for most apartments — it slides under the bed and costs little. Choose a hard-sided box instead if the tree shares space with heavy items or lives in a damp spot.

### How do I keep my Christmas tree from getting dusty in storage?

Use a zippered bag rather than an open bin, wrap the tree in an old sheet before it goes in, and lay a dust cover over the bag. Store it indoors — under the bed or in a closet — rather than on a balcony.

## Conclusion

Measure the tree, pick a bag or box that fits with room to spare, and give it one protected spot — under the bed, on the closet shelf, or standing in a closet corner — with the ornaments and lights packed beside it. Label everything, pack it the same day the tree comes down, and next December will start with decorating instead of untangling.
