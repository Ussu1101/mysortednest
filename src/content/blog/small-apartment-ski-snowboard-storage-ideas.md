Skis and snowboards are the hardest gear to store in a small apartment: long, awkward, and expensive, and leaning them in a corner by the door means scratched edges, tripped-over tails, and a hallway that never feels clean. These small apartment ski storage ideas get your gear off the floor, keep it dry, and leave it easy to grab on a powder morning. The 16 ideas below fit winter sports gear into a rental without drilling holes you will regret. You will find wall mounts, ceiling racks, freestanding stands, drying routines, and off-season hiding spots — all sized for apartments, not garages.

![Skis and a snowboard stored vertically on a pale wall in a bright apartment living room](/images/small-apartment-ski-snowboard-storage-ideas-hero.jpg)

## Measure the gear: length decides the whole system


### 1. Measure every ski and board, then add 6 inches of clearance

Lay each ski and board flat and measure tip to tail. Add roughly 6 inches for the mount hardware and room to lift gear in and out. A 170 cm ski needs a vertical wall run of about 73 inches, so check your ceiling height first.

### 2. Map the dead zones before you buy anything

Walk your apartment with a tape measure and find the spots that already go unused: the 8-inch gap beside the wardrobe, the wall above the shoe rack, the strip between the door frame and the corner. Check the gear clears light switches, radiators, and anything you open daily.

<div class="tip"><strong>Measure first, buy once:</strong> keep each ski and board length in your phone. Every rack listing shows a maximum length — checking it saves a return.</div>

## Wall mounts: the vertical ski rack that saves floor space


### 3. Install a padded two-arm wall mount for your daily pair

A two-arm wall mount holds one pair of skis or one snowboard flat against the wall, tips up, with rubber-coated arms that grip without scratching the topsheet. Mount it so the tails sit 3 to 4 inches off the floor, letting melting snow drip onto a small tray instead of your floorboards.

### 4. Stack two pairs on a horizontal wall rack

A horizontal rack with two sets of padded hooks stores two pairs of skis side by side, bindings facing out, at shoulder height. It keeps edges apart so they never clatter, and a neat row of skis looks intentional, like the wall art it replaces. Leave at least 2 inches between pairs so you can lift one out without dragging the other.

### 5. Use a wall-mounted board rack with a binding slot

Store a snowboard flat against the wall, bindings facing out, held by two arms that cradle the board below the bindings. Look for a rack with wider spacing where the bindings sit, so the board hangs level. One board per rack keeps the weight low; doubling up boards is how edges get chipped.

## Ceiling racks for boards and skis you use weekly


### 6. Hang a ceiling pulley hoist above the hallway

A pulley hoist lifts skis or a snowboard flat against the ceiling and lowers them with a rope when you need them. Mount it where nobody walks directly underneath, and keep the load under the hoist's rated weight — most handle 30 to 50 pounds, far more than any ski setup. The rope locks, so the gear stays put.

### 7. Build a simple ceiling sling from two padded straps

Two wide, padded straps screwed into ceiling joists make a hammock for a snowboard or a pair of skis. Space the straps about two-thirds of the gear's length apart, and pad contact points with foam pipe insulation so edges never touch bare metal. ## Freestanding racks for renters who cannot drill


### 8. Stand your skis in a freestanding vertical rack by the door

A freestanding vertical rack holds two to four pairs of skis upright, with padded slots that keep each pair separate. Place it in the entryway, 12 inches from the wall, so tails clear the skirting board and bindings never scrape paint. Put a boot tray underneath to catch meltwater. For more no-drill systems, see our [15 Small Apartment Storage Ideas for Renters](/blog/small-apartment-storage-ideas-renters-no-drilling/).

### 9. Use a tension-pole rack in a narrow gap

A floor-to-ceiling tension pole with ski arms fits into gaps as narrow as 10 inches — beside a wardrobe, behind a door, or at the end of a hallway. Twist the pole until it locks, then clip the arms on at the heights your gear needs. No holes, no adhesive, and it comes down in two minutes when you move.

## Boots, bindings, and poles: the accessory zone


### 10. Give boots their own ventilated shelf with a drip tray

Ski boots need airflow, not a sealed box. A wire shelf or slatted rack with a plastic drip tray underneath lets liners dry between trips and keeps salty meltwater off your floor. Store boots upside down or on their sides with the buckles open, and keep the tray near the door so wet boots never travel through the apartment.

### 11. Hang poles, helmets, and gloves on one entryway rail

A wall rail with S-hooks near the door holds poles by their straps, helmets by their vents, and gloves clipped together with a carabiner. Everything hangs at grab height, and the rail takes up 4 inches of wall depth. Keep gloves on the lowest hooks so they drip into the boot tray below, not onto your coats.

## Drying wet gear without wrecking the apartment


### 12. Wipe edges dry before the gear goes on the rack

Keep a microfiber towel by the door and wipe each edge and base the moment you come home. Thirty seconds prevents the rust spots that appear overnight on damp steel edges, and keeps slope salt and grit off your rack and floor. Do this before the gear goes vertical — once it is on the wall, you will not take it down to dry it.

### 13. Dry liners and gloves with a small fan, not a radiator

Pull boot liners out after every trip and set them upright in front of a small fan for a few hours. Radiators and hair dryers dry liners too fast and can warp the foam; a gentle fan dries them fully by morning. Stuff gloves with newspaper so they hold their shape, and replace it once it feels damp.

<div class="tip"><strong>The 10-minute rule:</strong> spend ten minutes drying and wiping gear the night you come home — gear stored wet on a Sunday has rusted edges and a smell you cannot remove by Friday.</div>

## Off-season storage: summer hiding spots for winter gear


### 14. Wax and bag skis before the summer hideaway

Before storing skis for the summer, wipe the bases clean, apply a thick coat of storage wax without scraping it off, and slide each pair into a padded ski bag. The wax stops the bases drying out, and the bag keeps dust and knocks away. Store boards the same way, bindings loosened slightly so the inserts are not under tension all summer. REI's expert guide to [storing skis and snowboards](https://www.rei.com/learn/expert-advice/how-to-store-skis-and-snowboards.html) covers the waxing and prep steps.

### 15. Slide bagged gear under the bed or on top of the wardrobe

A padded ski bag slides under most beds, and bagged skis fit on top of a wardrobe where nothing else goes. Both spots stay cool, dark, and out of the way all summer. Add a silica gel pack if your apartment gets humid, and never store gear in a hot attic or a damp basement corner. Our [18 Small Apartment Seasonal Storage Rotation Ideas](/blog/small-apartment-seasonal-storage-rotation-ideas/) shows how to run the twice-a-year swap that makes this routine automatic.

### 16. Label the bag so next winter starts in five minutes

Write the contents and the date on a luggage tag tied to the bag's handle: "Skis 170 cm, waxed April, edges checked." When the first snow forecast arrives, you grab the bag and go instead of opening three bags to find the right pair. Keep poles and the boot bag with the skis so the whole kit travels together.

## Mistakes to avoid

**Leaning skis loose in a corner.** Unsecured skis slide, fall, and chip edges — and land on whatever is nearby. Every pair needs a mount, a rack, or a bag, even if it is only for the night.

**Storing gear wet.** Wet edges rust in a day, wet liners smell in two, and wet gear drips onto your floor — and your downstairs neighbor's ceiling. Dry first, store second, every time.

**Hanging heavy gear from adhesive hooks.** Command-style hooks are rated for a few pounds, but skis with bindings weigh 15 to 20 pounds, and a board with bindings is heavier still. Adhesive hooks fail suddenly and drop your gear onto the floor.

**Buying a rack before measuring.** The most common waste of money is a beautiful rack 4 inches too short for your longest ski. Measure first, then match the rack's maximum length to your longest piece of gear.

**Storing gear against a radiator or in direct sun.** Heat dries out ski bases and warps snowboard cores over a summer — keep stored gear cool, dry, and out of direct sunlight.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I store skis in a small apartment without drilling holes?

Yes. Freestanding vertical racks, tension-pole racks, and over-the-door hooks all store skis without a single hole. A freestanding rack by the door handles two to four pairs, and a tension pole fits gaps as narrow as 10 inches. Both move out cleanly when your lease ends.

### How do I store a snowboard in an apartment?

Store it flat against the wall on a padded two-arm rack, bindings facing out, with the weight resting on the arms rather than the bindings. Keep it indoors, dry, and out of direct sunlight. For summer, wax the base, loosen the bindings, and slide it into a padded bag under the bed or on top of the wardrobe.

### What is the best way to dry ski boots in a small apartment?

Pull the liners out, open the buckles, and set boots and liners on a ventilated shelf with a drip tray in front of a small fan. They dry fully overnight. Avoid radiators and hair dryers, which warp liner foam, and never seal damp boots in a closed box or bag.

### How much wall space do I need for two pairs of skis?

A vertical mount for two pairs needs roughly 14 inches of wall width and a wall run taller than your longest ski plus 6 inches — about 73 inches for a 170 cm ski. A horizontal rack needs about 6 feet of wall width at shoulder height instead.

### Can I store skis on a balcony?

No. Balconies expose skis to rain, sun, and big temperature swings that dry out bases and rust edges, and most leases ban storing gear outside. Keep skis indoors, and use the balcony for gear that handles weather better.

## Conclusion

Good ski storage comes down to three things: measure your gear first, get it off the floor onto a wall or ceiling system that fits your lease, and dry everything before it goes away for the night.
